FPV reconnaissance robot, main body

Overview

The goal was a low-cost reconnaissance robot that could operate where a person should not walk in. The target use was search and rescue, specifically a remotely operated platform that lets an operator see, navigate, and assess a situation from a safe distance.

The system has two parts: a mobile robot body, and an operator-held controller that streams live FPV video and steers the camera. The robot drives on two 12V DC motors, lights dark space with a NeoPixel ring, and sends a live FPV feed to a screen on the remote. The camera sits on a two-axis gimbal. Servos follow the operator as they rotate the FPV screen.

The build came in at $390 against a $450 allowance, and met four of five required performance specifications.

What I built

I owned hardware wiring and physical integration on both the robot and the operator controller. That meant routing connections among the Arduino Mega, TB6612FNG motor driver, DC motors, transceiver, Bluetooth module, and the FPV camera and gimbal, working from the schematics the team drew.

I built the chassis, mounted the parts on the acrylic platform, and fit the drive wheels and rear stabilizer. I also set up the FPV system, from the camera paired to the screen on the controller through the two-axis gimbal. An MPU-6050 on the operator side tracks rotation of the FPV screen and sends orientation over Bluetooth to the Arduino Mega, which commands the servos to match. I handled procurement and the bill of materials, and stayed inside the budget after a motor failure and battery delays in the last week.

How it works

Robot body. An Arduino Mega is the controller. A FlySky transceiver takes drive commands from the RC remote and passes them to the TB6612FNG, which runs the two 12V DC motors for differential steering. An HC-06 Bluetooth module receives orientation from the operator and commands two servos to pan and tilt the FPV camera.

Operator controller. An Arduino Uno reads rotation from an MPU-6050 attached to the FPV screen. That data goes out over an HC-05 to the robot. When the operator tilts the screen, the camera follows. The screen gets a live analog video feed from the camera, separate from the Bluetooth link.
