Overview

Pittsburgh’s hills make a purely mechanical boost useful. This capstone built an energy recovery attachment that retrofits onto a standard bicycle. No electronics, no battery, no motor.

The system stores kinetic energy during downhill coasting by coiling a strong elastic band through a gear-driven mechanism linked to the rear wheel. A three-position lever switches between charging, neutral, and discharging. When the rider needs a burst of acceleration at a stop or on an uphill, a ratchet releases and the elastic uncoils, driving the rear wheel forward.

The project received Most Innovative at the CMU Design Expo in 2024.

What I built

I did most of the CAD, modeling the gear train, housing, ratchet assembly, and mounting interface in SolidWorks. The band gear, switching gears, drum, pawl, and acrylic housing walls were modeled by me and revised as the design moved.

I led fabrication of the prototype: laser-cut acrylic panels, the gear train, bearings and bushings, and the lever and ratchet. I also owned procurement and the budget, sourcing metric spur gears, hardware, and materials inside the team’s cost limit.

How it works

Charging. As the bike rolls downhill, the chain link to the rear wheel drives the gear train and coils the elastic band around the drum. A ratchet holds the stored energy.

Neutral. The switching gears leave the band gear. The ratchet holds the drum. The rider pedals with no drag from the system.

Discharging. The ratchet lifts and the switching gears re-engage with the direction reversed. The elastic uncoils, the drum spins, and the rear wheel is driven forward. The burst is short. It is meant for clearing an intersection or starting on an incline.
